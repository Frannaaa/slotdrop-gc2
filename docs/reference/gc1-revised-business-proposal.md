# SlotDrop | Revised GC1 business proposal

*Post-feedback revision of Group 3's original GC1 proposal · 16 November 2026*

Our original GC1 pitch was **SlotDrop — “Empty slot, better price.”** We wanted customers to find lower-priced appointments on short notice and providers to earn from empty or cancelled slots. Ma'am thought the two-sided problem was worth exploring, but she was right to question the supply: a popular provider may not have spare appointments, and discounting an available seat does not help if that customer would otherwise have paid full price.

This revision keeps the original GC1 concept and corrects its weak points. It describes the **proposed business**, while [`business.md`](../business.md) shows what the team actually built in GC2. It does not replace the historically submitted GC1 or claim signed studio partners, measured demand or earned commercial revenue.

## 1. Business concept brief

### Name, niche and promise

**SlotDrop** is a two-sided booking marketplace for **released, short-notice seats in sessions that providers have already decided to run**. For the first pilot, we would focus on beginner-friendly creative workshops around Katipunan and nearby Quezon City—not every salon, gym and activity in Metro Manila.

For a customer, the promise is straightforward: *find something you can still do in the next three days, see the final price and deadline, and book it in one place*. For a studio, it is a way to test whether a few genuinely unsold seats can bring in additional contribution without undermining normal bookings.

**E-commerce category:** B2C services/experiences marketplace with B2B supplier participation, booking inventory and per-transaction commission. The physical class happens at a studio; discovery, price selection, booking and confirmation happen online.

### Who we would serve

| Side | Initial profile | Behavior and motivation | What we still need to verify |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Primary customers** | Proposed **22–35** year-old working adults around Katipunan, UP Village and Teachers Village, QC; initial screening hypothesis **₱35,000–₱80,000 gross monthly income** | Plans an occasional date, friend activity or free afternoon on short notice; values ease and upfront group prices; willing to compare deals but needs assurance that the class will really run | Whether this group actually buys ₱2,500–₱3,000 workshops, how frequently, what travel radius they accept and whether short notice is desirable |
| **Secondary customers** | Students and young adults in the same catchment area | Looks for social, beginner-friendly experiences; more price-sensitive, often plans with friends | A separate lower-budget offer may be necessary; we should not assume a ₱2,565 workshop is student-affordable |
| **Providers** | Independently operated workshop studios with regularly scheduled classes and observable unfilled capacity | Prefer filling otherwise empty seats, preserving brand/pricing control and avoiding the cost of creating an extra class | Real seat vacancy, material costs, cancellation cutoffs, minimum net payout and whether they will release inventory |

Research candidates include **Laro Ceramics, Bumi & Ashe, AMT Ceramics, Tahanan Pottery and Sip & Gogh**. The team reviewed public listings; **none is presented as a signed SlotDrop partner**.

### The problem—without overpromising

Customers may find activities through separate studio websites, social posts and booking pages, with different requirements and deadlines. A provider may have unsold places in a class that is already going ahead. Those two problems sometimes overlap, but not always: highly sought-after sessions might already be full, and a vacancy may occur too late for the studio to prepare.

Our original wording suggested guaranteed access to popular providers and guaranteed attendance. That was too strong. The revised offer is **conditional on a provider confirming a real allocation**, its price, its booking cutoff and whether the session will run. We would rather show fewer trustworthy options than imply every cancellation becomes an instant discounted booking.

### Why choose SlotDrop?

A customer should see **which specific near-term seats are open, the studio-set cutoff, remaining allocation, inclusions and complete peso amount** before giving contact details. A provider should be able to release or pause a small allotment instead of discounting an entire class. The differentiator is the **combination of local focus, explicit released capacity, transparent cutoffs and studio payout discipline**. A last-minute marketplace itself is not new.

## 2. Strategic analysis — SWOT and Business Model Canvas

We retain the **two frameworks used in the original GC1**, but connect each to a decision rather than leaving it as a diagram.

### Framework A: SWOT, with responses

| | Business reality | Our response |
|---|---|---|
| **Strength** | A narrow, understandable job: rescue bookable capacity already paid for in a scheduled class. The provider controls allocation and deadlines. | Start with one workshop type and one supplier, not a broad marketplace with thin inventory. |
| **Weakness** | Two-sided cold start, low brand trust, small per-order commission, and no live connection to a studio calendar. | Confirm a fixed allocation and reconcile it manually each day before accepting real bookings; test one neighborhood. |
| **Opportunity** | Local experience discovery and group bookings may make incremental use of spare seats without requiring a studio to run another class. | Test predictable weekend/weekday release patterns and buddy pricing as separate experiments. |
| **Threat** | **Ghost slots**, providers unwilling to discount, **deal-only customers**, displaced full-price sales, and established booking channels. | Set aside inventory with the partner, agree on a minimum provider payout, track direct-channel sales, and pause offers where incremental profit is not demonstrated. |

These responses are **pilot operating proposals**. GC2 only protects the seats *inside SlotDrop*. It cannot verify whether another provider system has sold the same physical seat.

### Framework B: Business Model Canvas

| Block | Proposed operating model |
|---|---|
| **Customer segments** | QC adults planning an occasional short-notice creative activity; secondary student segment; providers with recurring spare seats |
| **Value propositions** | Customers: transparent near-term bookable options. Providers: measured incremental contribution from controlled releases |
| **Channels** | Studio referral posts, local community groups, search/social discovery and the SlotDrop web marketplace; test channel cost rather than assuming free reach |
| **Customer relationships** | Clear policies, saved booking reference, provider-backed fulfillment and support for discrepancies; accounts and self-service cancellations are later features |
| **Revenue streams** | Proposed **10% studio-paid commission on accepted bookings**; no extra charge to the demo customer. Featured placement is a possible *future* product, not launch revenue |
| **Key resources** | Supply agreements, credible class data and photography, reliable transactional booking software, local distribution and customer support |
| **Key activities** | Recruit and verify studios, agree allocations/cutoffs, keep listings fresh, acquire customers, confirm bookings, reconcile supply and disputes |
| **Key partners** | Pilot studio(s), hosting/database providers, and later payment/notification/calendaring vendors subject to signed agreements |
| **Cost structure** | Customer acquisition, support, studio onboarding, hosting, refunds/failed bookings, payment processing (future), reconciliation and founder labor |

**Assessment:** The concept is technically achievable at small scale, but its constraint is **supply quality and incremental economics**, not whether we can build a booking page. The model fails if studios offer only unattractive seats, demand does not convert before cutoff, or discounted sales replace buyers who would otherwise pay direct.

## 3. Competitor landscape and positioning

We distinguish alternatives by **the job they actually perform**, not by calling every brand a direct competitor.

### Direct competitors: customers can book an experience or monetized spare slot through them

| Player | Where it overlaps | What it does well | Where SlotDrop would have to prove an advantage |
|---|---|---|---|
| [**Klook**](https://www.klook.com/en-PH/activity/116888-pottery-workshop-wabi-sabi/) | Bookable local activities and workshop discovery | Distribution, established customer flow and experience listings | Better **verified last-72-hour QC seats** and clear per-session inventory/cutoffs, not an unsupported claim of cheaper prices |
| [**ClassPass**](https://classpass.com/partners/faqs) | Monetizing excess class/appointment capacity | Existing audience, inventory tools, negotiated payout floors | Simple peso-denominated per-booking purchase for **creative workshops**, without requiring customers to understand subscription credits |
| [**Fresha**](https://www.fresha.com/pricing) | Original GC1's broader salon/spa appointment booking space | Scheduling software and direct/marketplace customer acquisition | Our narrowed workshop pilot avoids trying to displace a full salon operating system; it is only partly overlapping at launch |

ClassPass is particularly instructive: it already addresses surplus capacity and has provider payout protections. We cannot reasonably claim to have invented that approach. The practical challenge is whether a *simpler, QC-focused workshop offering* is worth a supplier's additional operating effort.

### Indirect competitors and substitutes

| Alternative | Why a customer or provider would use it instead | Implication |
|---|---|---|
| **Studio-direct booking** — [Laro](https://laro-ceramics.com/products/mug-making-class-manila), [Bumi & Ashe](https://bumiandashe.com/products/mug-making-workshop-1), [Tahanan](https://tahananpottery.ph/) | Official schedule, studio relationship, sometimes existing friend discounts; studio keeps the direct sale without a marketplace commission | Bring genuinely new demand rather than merely redirecting full-price customers |
| **Other leisure choices** — cafés, cinemas, malls and low-cost group activities | Compete for the same spontaneous free time and disposable income | Explain inclusions and value; pricing must justify the whole outing |

### Auxiliary discovery channels and cross-industry benchmarks

[Google Maps](https://maps.google.com/) and Instagram/Facebook posts help people **find** studios, but are not themselves proof of live seat inventory or completed booking. They are more realistically **acquisition channels and discovery substitutes** than identical booking competitors. [HotelTonight](https://www.hoteltonight.com/faq) is a relevant **surplus-inventory analogy** from hotels, not a QC workshop competitor.

**Positioning test:** *“Which workshop can two friends still attend nearby, what will they pay together, and how late can they book?”* SlotDrop needs to answer that faster and more reliably than visiting multiple provider pages. If the providers do not release real seats, the advantage disappears.

Detailed public source notes and studio comparisons are in [business-research-appendix.md](business-research-appendix.md).

## 4. Monetization, features and tech stack

This section was **missing from our original GC1 submission**; it is now explicit.

### Who pays, and how much?

SlotDrop proposes to deduct a **10% platform commission from the amount paid for each confirmed booking**. In the GC2 demo the quote, fee and provider payout are computed and persisted in Philippine centavos; payments are simulated. The 10% is a **business-model assumption**, not a negotiated partner rate. Paid featured placements are deferred because a new marketplace has not yet earned enough traffic or trust to sell visibility.

Example based on a researched Laro Ceramics listing at **₱3,000** and simulated release prices:

| Booking | Customer pays | SlotDrop 10% fee | Provider payout before attendee costs |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| One standard seat at ₱2,700 | ₱2,700 | ₱270 | ₱2,430 |
| Two standard seats | ₱5,400 | ₱540 | ₱4,860 |
| Two buddy seats at ₱2,565 each | ₱5,130 | ₱513 | ₱4,617 |

**The difficult question is not the fee calculation; it is displacement.** If a seat would have remained empty and serving that guest costs an assumed **₱500**, the provider adds **₱1,930** of contribution on a standard booking. If the same buyer would have paid ₱3,000 directly, the studio instead gives up **₱570** of gross proceeds. We must check total bookings across *all* channels.

**Buddy-price decision.** A two-seat buddy booking gives up **₱243 of studio payout** relative to two standard seats but adds **₱1,687 of incremental studio contribution** (after an assumed extra ₱500 attendee cost) if it brings a second person who would not have come. The modeled break-even incremental-buddy share is **12.6%**. Both figures are sensitivities, not measured buyer behavior.

**Four-week hypothetical pilot:** 16 classes × 4 released seats = 64 offered. Assume 24 standard and 8 buddy seats sold (28 bookings): gross booking value **₱85,320**; platform commission **₱8,532**; provider payout **₱76,788**. Assuming ₱500 incremental cost per guest, eight displaced direct customers, ₱2,500 provider coordination, ₱50 support per booking and ₱6,000 platform outreach/ops, illustrative provider incremental contribution is **₱38,288** and platform surplus **₱1,132**, **before** founder labor, taxes, payment fees and refunds. That margin is thin and could reverse quickly.

### Core e-commerce features

| Feature | Why customers/providers need it | Where it sits |
|---|---|---|
| Public workshop marketplace and filters | Compare a small set of live-eligible sessions | Home, Browse, Detail; `GET /api/slots` |
| Provider-controlled released-seat allocations | Studio chooses exactly which capacity it risks on SlotDrop | Owner's offer controls and `slots` database |
| Standard/buddy tier pricing | Show real party totals and test whether group offers add attendees | Selection + server quote; `GET /api/pricing/quote` |
| Simulated booking and checkout | Complete the e-commerce transaction without collecting money in GC2 | Checkout; `POST /api/bookings` |
| Saved receipt and inventory lock | Prevent phantom success, overselling and duplicate booking retries | PostgreSQL transaction; Confirmation `GET /api/bookings/:id` |
| Owner metrics | Track bookings, fees, average booking amount, popular activity and almost-full releases | Owner; `GET /api/metrics` |

### Stack and integrations

**GC2 implemented:** Plain **HTML/CSS/JavaScript** customer pages; **Node.js 22 + Express 5** for JSON APIs and validation; **PostgreSQL 16** for relational records, booking locks and immutable saved price snapshots; **Render + Neon** for hosting/persistent database; GitHub Actions and Postman for testing. This is a real browser → server → DB architecture. The browser does not choose the accepted fee or write to PostgreSQL directly.

**Future options, not claimed as installed:** React for the Final Project front end; supplier accounts/roles; **PayMongo or Xendit** for live payments, notifications, Google Maps/geolocation, and provider calendar integration. The original GC1 named Supabase Auth as a possible integration; GC2 deliberately implements no login because the course MVP does not require it. Payment-provider selection and commercial terms are unconfirmed.

## 5. Product architecture and information flow

`Customer website / Owner interface
            ↓ HTTPS JSON requests
Node.js + Express routes
            ↓ input validation, discovery, quote, booking rules
PostgreSQL repositories + transaction-safe seat locks
            ↓
providers → services → slots → bookings
               ↘ provider_applications
            ↑ saved receipt / metrics
Confirmation page + Owner dashboard`

The **data is organized by business entity, not separate customer/provider/admin databases**. Each provider has services; each service has released offers; a confirmed booking references one offer and snapshots the accepted price, fee, quantity and studio payout.

**End-to-end booking:** Browse fetches eligible released sessions → Detail opens one slot ID → Selection requests the server's party quote → Checkout submits a stable request ID and accepted total → the server locks the slot row, checks remaining seats and recalculates money → one booking is committed → Confirmation reloads that saved booking by ID → Owner reads the same data. Unknown items produce 404; invalid input 400; insufficient stock or stale quote 409. A matching retry returns the original receipt.

**Important boundary:** GC2's allocation lock prevents double-selling *inside SlotDrop*, not on a studio's own website. A real pilot needs a ring-fenced allocation or integrated shared calendar. There is no live payment gateway, customer account, refund processor or off-platform inventory sync.

## 8. Pilot design, acceptance gates and limits

**Before the pilot:** Ask one studio for four weeks of session capacity, sales and cancellation records; confirm prep lead times, actual extra-attendee costs, minimum viable payout, and explicit inventory consent. Customer interviews should test whether the screened age/income/location segment finds a last-minute workshop attractive.

**Weeks 1–2:** Release a small, studio-approved number of seats for sessions already going ahead at one standard price. Record direct and SlotDrop sales together; reconcile allocations daily. Pause immediately if a displayed seat is not actually available.

**Weeks 3–4:** Test buddy pricing for comparable sessions, not all sessions indiscriminately. Record whether bookings added people or displaced existing full-price buyers. Compare total studio contribution and SlotDrop net commission after real support and acquisition costs.

**Decision:** Expand only if the studio consistently has inventory *before* cutoff, customers convert, seat reconciliation stays accurate, both sides retain positive incremental economics, and the supplier wants to continue. If not, change the offer timing/price or stop. A functional demo is evidence of **technical feasibility**, not proof of **product-market fit**.
